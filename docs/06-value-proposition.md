# Value proposition & positioning

## Question
Why join, why pay, why trust a neutral convener?

## Findings

### How to read this document
- [assumption] This is a synthesis of documents 02-05, written 2026-11-04 with no new research and no customer interviews. Every proposition below rests on a pain, archetype or competitor finding already documented; the persuasive step from finding to "why join" is judgment and is labeled `[estimated]` or `[assumption]`, never `[sourced]`.
- [assumption] The strongest evidence for the shared pain, permit-time and fee variance, comes from operator trade groups with a policy agenda (doc 03; S-038, S-039). No buyer, contractor or ROW manager has been asked whether this pain is worth paying to reduce.
- [assumption] Doc 05 found that prediction and a three-sided structure are each not unique. This document therefore claims only a combination and a neutrality position, and lists what has to be proven before either is relied on.

### Value proposition by member side
- [estimated] Summary of the three propositions; each row names the pain and archetype from doc 03 and the supporting finding from docs 03-05:

| Side | Archetype (doc 03) | Pain (doc 03) | Proposition | Supporting finding | Pays? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Operators | O1 national carrier, O2 regional fiber builder; O3 small telco gets a lighter regional view | Cannot predict permit time or fees by jurisdiction | Jurisdiction-level permit-time and fee benchmarks, predicted approval dates, a requirements library, and peer meetings with ROW managers | Approval times from days to over a year, varying between neighbors [S-038]; no benchmark product in the operator associations (doc 05) | Yes, dues plus optional dashboard subscription |
| Contractors and engineering firms | C1 national prime, C2 regional contractor, C3 engineering and permitting firm | Do not know where work will start or when permits will clear; re-research each jurisdiction | Early visibility of builds by region, permit-time predictions for bid pricing, a maintained requirements and fee library | Capex timing drives contractor results [S-043]; FBA vendor dues of $6,000 show contractors already pay for association access [S-029] | Yes, dues plus optional subscription |
| ROW managers | R1 municipal or county ROW manager, R2 state DOT permits office | Small staff, high permit volume, no advance view of planned builds, no peer benchmarks | Advance view of planned builds in their area, cross-jurisdiction benchmarks on fees and timelines, model ordinances and peer meetings, free or near-free | Agency permit platforms show only agency-level data (doc 05); agencies already pay $925-$2,125 to NATOA [S-030] | No, free; they contribute data |

- [estimated] Operators: the proposition is "know before you file." The pain is variance and opacity of permit timing and fees across jurisdictions (doc 03). USTelecom reports approvals from a few days to more than a year and variation between neighboring jurisdictions [S-038], and the Fiber Broadband Association describes inconsistent, paper-based permitting [S-039]. Operators would be buying a way to plan crews and grant deadlines around that variance. This is inferred from advocacy filings and needs operator interviews.
- [sourced] Fiber Broadband Association network-operator dues are $1,200, $2,250 or $6,000 a year by subscriber count [S-027]
- [estimated] Operators therefore already have an association-dues budget line of this size, which is the price range a membership offer will be compared with.
- [estimated] Small operators (O3) are unlikely to value an analytics seat (doc 03) and already pay NTCA, WISPA or state dues, so the realistic offer to them is a low-cost regional view and meetings, not the full dashboard. The paying core is O1 and O2.
- [estimated] Contractors and engineering firms: the proposition is "see the work coming." Doc 03 infers that large contractors value predicted build and capex timing more than permit benchmarks, because Dycom ties its results to customers' capital budgets and approval timing [S-043]. Engineering firms (C3) would use the jurisdiction requirements and approval-time history to quote clients, but their jurisdiction knowledge is their product, so the license must allow client deliverables. Both points are inferences and not stated by a contractor.
- [sourced] Dycom's three largest customers accounted for 25.4%, 14.0% and 10.8% of fiscal 2026 contract revenue [S-043]
- [estimated] Because of that concentration, a prime is likely to ask whether its main carrier customers have joined before it does (doc 04); this is an inference, not stated by a contractor.
- [estimated] ROW managers: the proposition is "see what is coming and compare yourself with peers, for free." Doc 03 documents their pain as permit volume against small staff, shot clocks and conflicts with capital projects [S-041] [S-042]. Doc 05 found that agency permitting platforms such as Accela and OpenGov offer agency-level views and no cross-jurisdiction comparison. A benchmark across jurisdictions is therefore a benefit their own software does not give them, and it is the reason they would contribute data. Whether it is valuable enough to justify staff time and records-law exposure is untested.
- [sourced] NATOA charges local-government agencies $925-$2,125 a year by population [S-030]
- [sourced] Hidalgo County, TX was invoiced $1,630 for three NATOA staff seats in October 2023 [S-031]
- [estimated] Together these suggest local governments do pay modest association dues, so a nominal fee is not unprecedented.
- [estimated] Cross-side benefit: each side's data makes the product better for the others. Operators and contractors buy because ROW managers contribute permit records; ROW managers contribute because they get benchmarks and advance notice that their own software lacks. This loop is the reason for a three-sided body, and it is a hypothesis that no buyer has confirmed.

### Neutral-convener positioning
- [estimated] Positioning statement (draft for testing): "THE ROW is the independent association where the people who build in public rights-of-way, the people who build for them and the people who manage the rights-of-way share one set of permit, fee and build facts, and none of the three sides controls it." Persuasion, not evidence; wording is to be tested in interviews.
- [sourced] The Common Ground Alliance board's stakeholder directors each represent one of seventeen stakeholder categories [S-058]
- [estimated] The Common Ground Alliance is the closest structural precedent found: a board with seats for many stakeholder categories, including public works.
- [sourced] NATOA's eligibility includes staff managing poles, conduits or rights-of-way, and it charges for-profit associates $1,970 a year [S-030]
- [estimated] NATOA therefore already combines local-government ROW staff and industry associates in one association, so mixed membership has precedent.
- [estimated] The objection each side will raise is the same one in doc 03: R1 and R2 fear industry lobbying in disguise, while O1 and O2 fear that contributed data helps rivals and that a body including cities will lobby against fees and shot clocks. Fee and shot-clock rules are contested in public proceedings [S-040], which is why the governance has to be written down before launch.
- [estimated] Doc 05 judged that the neutral benchmark is the defensible part of the gap because FBA's permitting study argues for fee caps and shot clocks against jurisdictions, so ROW managers are unlikely to treat its data as neutral [S-055]
- [assumption] Governance and trust mechanisms the association would need; all are proposals, none is validated, and none is sourced except where a precedent is cited:

| Mechanism | Proposed rule | Why (doc) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Board composition | Operators, contractors and ROW managers each hold an equal block of seats; no side holds a majority; large-carrier and large-prime seats are limited so no single paying customer controls the board | Doc 05: neutrality is a differentiator only if ROW managers have real weight, as in CGA's stakeholder board [S-058] | [assumption] |
| Lobbying | No positions on contested issues such as permit fees, shot clocks or gross-revenue ROW fees; no government-affairs advocacy by the association | Doc 03: these issues divide the sides [S-040] | [assumption] |
| Tax treatment | Because a 501(c)(6) must disclose the lobbying share of dues [S-044], a no-lobbying rule would let the association state a lobbying share near zero, which is a selling point to buyers who currently see 20% as non-deductible at NATOA [S-031] | Doc 04 | [estimated] |
| Data contribution terms | Ask agencies only for fields they already publish or would release on request (permit type, street-level location, application and issue dates, status, fees); no fee; a periodic export; named staff owner; no exclusivity and no agency indemnity; termination at any time | Doc 04: keep approval light | [assumption] |
| Operator data | Operators do not contribute build plans in the first version; any operator contribution is aggregated and not identifiable to agencies or competitors | Doc 03 (O1 will not share build plans); doc 04 | [assumption] |
| Public-records handling | Treat data sent by agencies as probably a public record; give agencies aggregated views and no operator-identifiable build plans; counsel to review each state's law | Doc 04, reading of Washington's definition of a public record [S-037] and Florida's narrow exemption [S-036] | [estimated] |
| Methodology transparency | Publish how benchmarks and predictions are built and how accurate they have been, so members can check them | Doc 05: Shovels' pages describe no forecasting method or accuracy [S-062] | [assumption] |
| Separation of dues and data | Membership is dues; the dashboard is a separate subscription with an order form | Doc 04: lets each go to its own budget owner | [estimated] |

- [estimated] A no-lobbying rule has a cost: operators that join an association largely for policy influence will find less to buy, and ROW managers who want a voice against operator pressure will find the same. The proposition works only if enough members value a neutral data source more than advocacy. Doc 03 shows existing associations such as USTelecom, NTCA and NATOA already serve the advocacy need.
- [assumption] Who sits on the board in practice, how seats are elected and how disputes over data are settled are not designed here. They should be drafted after interviews show which seat allocations each side would accept.

### Why a member would pay for predictions, and the limits
- [sourced] CostQuest's Fiber Forecast Model scores each unserved location for likelihood of fiber deployment in the next six months [S-067]
- [estimated] Prediction of fiber builds is therefore not unique to this idea (doc 05).
- [sourced] Shovels calls itself "The prediction engine for the built world" while its pages describe no forecasting methodology [S-062]
- [sourced] Shovels sells permit-data plans at $599 and $999 a month [S-032]
- [sourced] CGA runs the DIRT damage database and a Damage Prevention Dashboard combining 811-center and DIRT data [S-059]
- [sourced] CGA's board has stakeholder directors for seventeen stakeholder categories [S-058]
- [estimated] CGA is therefore an existing multi-stakeholder body with a data product in damage prevention, so a three-sided structure is not unique.
- [sourced] FBA commissioned a Cartesian study of more than 100 permitting examples across 71 local jurisdictions [S-055]
- [estimated] Permit benchmarking by an operator association therefore already exists, though as a one-off study rather than a continuously updated benchmark.
- [estimated] Reasons a member could still pay, in descending order of confidence, each a judgment and not a finding:
  1. A permit-time and fee benchmark that agencies helped build and that all sides accept as neutral. This is the part doc 05 judged defensible, because Shovels has no government-side membership and FBA argues for operators.
  2. A single membership that bundles the benchmark with meetings with ROW managers, which no vendor sells and which NATOA and FBA sell separately to their own sides (doc 05).
  3. Predictions that are built on agency-contributed permit and plan data that vendors without government relationships cannot get. This holds only if agencies contribute at scale and quality, which is the main unproven point in the design spec.
  4. Predictions for contractors of where operators will build and when, combined with permit-time predictions. CostQuest forecasts builds by location and Shovels reports permit patterns, but neither page reviewed combines the two for a contractor's bid (doc 05).
- [estimated] What is not a reason to pay: "we predict" alone. A buyer who wants permit intelligence can buy Shovels at about $7,200-$12,000 a year [S-032], and a buyer who wants a fiber build forecast can use CostQuest [S-067]. Marketing the dashboard as a prediction engine would put it against two vendors that already say so.
- [estimated] Price anchors from doc 04 frame the ask: dues cluster at $1,000-$10,000 and self-serve permit data runs $7,000-$12,000 a year, so a membership above about $10,000 will be judged as a data purchase and compared with Shovels. That comparison favors the dashboard only if its predictions are demonstrably more accurate or cover something Shovels does not, such as fees, requirements and agency-verified data.
- [estimated] Honest statement of what remains differentiated: not prediction, and not three-sided governance in general (CGA), but the combination of (a) ROW managers as full members and data contributors, (b) cross-jurisdiction permit-time and fee benchmarks with published methods and (c) modeled forecasts, sold as one neutral membership. Doc 05 found no organization reviewed that combines all three, and noted that each piece exists elsewhere and that incumbents could add the missing pieces within one to two years.
- [estimated] The differentiation is therefore a position that has to be earned by recruiting agency data and demonstrating accuracy, not a feature that cannot be copied. If FBA licensed Shovels or CostQuest data, or NATOA added an operator tier, the neutral-convener claim would be the only remaining difference (doc 05).
- [assumption] Predictions only justify a price premium if they are shown to be accurate against held-out outcomes; no accuracy test has been run, and no prediction method has been specified in this research.

### What we would have to validate before launch
- [assumption] Interviews, at least four per side as doc 03 recommends. Operators (O1, O2, O3): is permit-time and fee variance costly enough to pay to reduce, who owns the budget, would they pay for a benchmark or only for meetings, and would they accept a no-lobbying association?
- [assumption] Interviews with contractors and engineering firms (C1, C2, C3): do they want build and permit predictions for bids, would they pay if their main carrier customers had not joined, and can the license work for client deliverables and for firms whose jurisdiction knowledge is their product?
- [assumption] Interviews with ROW managers (R1, R2, R3): would they contribute permit fields on a regular export, who would approve it, what do records-law and security reviews require, and would they believe an association funded by operators and contractors is neutral?
- [assumption] Willingness to pay: test a $1,000-$10,000 membership and a separate $7,000-$12,000 dashboard subscription against the published comparables, including the question of whether buyers would rather buy Shovels or an FBA dashboard.
- [assumption] Data feasibility: obtain permit data from a pilot set of jurisdictions in one region and test whether it is complete and consistent enough for benchmarks, and whether it supports a prediction that beats a simple historical average.
- [assumption] Governance acceptance: show each side the proposed board blocks, no-lobbying rule and data terms and record which seat allocations they accept. Take the draft to counsel for the 501(c)(6) lobbying disclosure rule and state records law.
- [assumption] Competitive response: ask FBA, NATOA and the vendors' customers what they would do if a neutral benchmark were launched, and check Shovels and CostQuest capabilities with demos, which this desk research could not do.

## Assumptions
- Permit-time and fee variance is a pain buyers will pay to reduce; the only evidence is operator advocacy (S-038, S-039) and no buyer has been interviewed.
- ROW managers will contribute permit data regularly and will accept a free or near-free membership funded by operators and contractors if they get cross-jurisdiction benchmarks their software does not give them.
- Operators and contractors will accept a no-lobbying association and will pay for neutral data rather than advocacy.
- Equal board blocks and the governance mechanisms listed above are enough for ROW managers to treat the association as neutral; they have not been tested.
- Agency data contributed at scale makes predictions better than predictions built from public records by vendors such as Shovels or CostQuest.
- Predictions can be made accurate enough to justify a price above self-serve permit data; no method or accuracy test exists yet.
- The combination of ROW managers as members, cross-jurisdiction benchmarks and modeled forecasts remains unoccupied long enough to establish the association; doc 05 expects incumbents could close the gap within one to two years.
